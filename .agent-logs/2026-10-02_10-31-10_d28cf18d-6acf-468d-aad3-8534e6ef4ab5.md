# 2. PRODUCT PRINCIPLES

Use these principles to evaluate the product.

These are not instructions to clone Amazon.

They are product-quality heuristics derived from established ecommerce usability research and the actual Amazon/Flipkart reconnaissance already completed for this project.

## Product discovery

A user should be able to:

```text
arrive
→ understand what the store sells
→ search or browse
→ narrow the catalogue
→ compare products
→ inspect a product
→ understand price/availability
→ purchase
```

Search and product listing UX are particularly important because users need to efficiently narrow large catalogues and compare products. Baymard's current research identifies filtering, sorting, applied-filter visibility, result scannability, and mobile filtering as recurring ecommerce usability problems.

## Product page

The PDP must answer the user's purchase questions without forcing unnecessary navigation.

At minimum:

- what is it?
- what does it look like?
- how much does it cost?
- what variant am I buying?
- is it available?
- when will I receive it?
- what exactly am I getting?
- how do I purchase it?

Established ecommerce research treats product information, imagery, options, availability, price, and clear add-to-cart feedback as core PDP responsibilities.

Do not add low-value PDP features simply because Amazon has them.

## Cost transparency

Users should not be surprised by the total cost late in the journey.

Where the product model supports it, surface a reasonable shipping/cost estimate near the purchase decision and make the cart/checkout total understandable. Baymard specifically identifies total-order-cost visibility as an important PDP and cart issue.

## Cart

The cart is not just a storage page.

It should allow users to:

- understand what they selected
- verify variant/quantity
- change quantity
- remove items
- recover accidental removal where appropriate
- understand subtotal
- understand shipping/other costs
- understand savings if applicable
- proceed confidently

Do not allow recommendation modules to overpower the transaction.

## Checkout

Checkout is a trust and error-recovery flow.

The user should always understand:

- where they are
- what they are buying
- what they are paying
- what information is required
- what happens next
- whether an error occurred
- how to recover from the error

Baymard's checkout research consistently identifies checkout flow and form usability as major sources of abandonment. Its 2025 benchmark reports substantial room for improvement across desktop and mobile checkout UX.

## Mobile

Mobile is not a compressed desktop layout.

The user has:

- less context
- less visible content
- touch interaction
- keyboard occlusion
- more scrolling

Therefore:

- preserve orientation
- keep primary actions discoverable
- minimize unnecessary typing
- use appropriate input types
- keep critical totals visible
- avoid unnecessary modal chains
- prevent horizontal overflow
- make filter/sort interaction deliberate

Baymard's mobile ecommerce research specifically highlights the loss of overview, filter complexity, product-page scanning, and checkout form constraints on small screens.
